2. The STAMP CARD Framework

Each letter in STAMP CARD represents one category of grammar error tested in the O-Level editing question. Think of it like a bubble tea stamp card: go through the letters one by one for every numbered line, check each category, and stamp off errors as you find them until you collect all 8 corrections.

2.1 The Nine Categories

The framework provides nine categories to check on every numbered line. The exam includes eight errors in total across the passage, with two lines deliberately error-free. The framework letters are the categories you check; they do not map one-to-one onto the eight errors.

Error TypeQuick Check Question
SSubject-Verb AgreementDoes the verb agree with the subject in number?
TTenseIs the verb in the correct tense? Check time markers.
A1ArticlesIs ‘a’, ‘an’ or ‘the’ used correctly? Based on sound, not spelling.
MMatch PronounsDoes the pronoun match its antecedent in number, gender and form?
PPrepositionsIs the correct preposition used? Check fixed phrases and collocations.
CConnectorsIs the linking word logically correct? ‘Although’ and ‘but’ cannot be used together.
A2Adjective / Adverb (Word Form)Is it describing a noun (adjective) or a verb / adjective / adverb (adverb, usually -ly)?
RRight NumberIs the noun singular or plural? Does the quantifier match?
DDegree of ComparisonComparative or superlative correct? An adjective takes either -er/-est or more/most, never both.

Note on the two A’s

STAMP CARD contains two A categories. A1 is Articles (a, an, the). A2 is Adjective / Adverb (word form). They test entirely different things — do not confuse them. When working through the checklist, treat them as two separate stops.

2.1.1 Apply the STAMP CARD procedure to a passage

Technique: Use STAMP CARD as a checklist for every numbered line. Read the passage once for meaning first, then check each letter in order, prioritising T, S, A2.

  1. Before reading a single word, write S-T-A-M-P-C-A-R-D in the margin.
  2. Read the entire passage once for meaning. Identify the tense and topic. Do not correct yet.
  3. For every numbered line, work through each letter in order. Stop the moment you find the error — each line has at most one.
  4. Write your correction clearly. Tick error-free lines. Never leave any line blank.

Thought Process — Why T, S, A2

T, S and A2 errors appear in almost every exam passage because they involve the most common grammar rules in English: tense consistency, agreement, and word class. Starting with these three means you will catch the majority of errors efficiently before spending time on the less frequent categories. Treat T, S and A2 as the first pass over every line. (Note: this is A2, word form — not A1, articles, which is tested less often.)

3. Error Types in Detail

Each error type is explained with the full grammar rule, all known sub-patterns, worked examples, and annotated thought-process boxes that explain how an expert reader identifies and corrects each error.

3.1 Tense Errors T

3.1.1 The Rule

The tense of every verb must match the time frame the passage is set in. Narrative and recount passages use the past tense. Informative and factual passages use the present tense. Your first read of the passage exists to establish the tense.

Time MarkerSignalsExpected Tense
yesterday, last year, in 2010, once, at that time, in the pastPast events — completedPast Simple throughout
every day, now, currently, generally, always, nowadaysPresent facts or habitsPresent Simple throughout
by 2030, in the future, next year, soon, eventuallyPredictions or future stateswill + base verb

The three high-yield tenses. Past Simple, Present Simple and Present Perfect are the only tenses heavily tested in editing. Learn these three thoroughly before the others.

TenseFormWhen to UseExample
Past Simpleverb+ed / irregularCompleted action; narrative passages.She walked.
Present Simpleverb / verb+sHabits, routines, general truths, factual passages.She walks.
Present Perfecthave/has + p.p.Past action with continuing relevance. Tested via clash with specific past time markers.She has walked.

For the full twelve-tense reference, see Appendix A.

3.1.2 Identify the wrong tense

  1. Read the full passage once. Identify the tense from time markers or context.
  2. For each numbered line, identify the main verb.
  3. Ask — does this verb match the passage tense?
  4. If not, correct it to the appropriate tense form.

Worked Example 1

She walk into the lab yesterday.→She walked into the lab yesterday.

Time marker: yesterday — signals past. Main verb walk is the base form. Correct to Past Simple: walked.

Worked Example 2

I have seen that film last night.→I saw that film last night.

Last night is a specific past time, which locks the event in the past. Present Perfect cannot co-exist with it. Replace with Past Simple.

Worked Example 3

The problem is compounding.→The problem is compounded.

Ask: is the subject doing an action, or in a condition? The problem is in a worsened state, so the past participle is required, not the present participle.

Thought Process — Present vs Past Participle After ‘To Be’

When you see is/was/are/were followed by a verb form, ask: is the subject doing something, or in a condition?

  • Doing something → present participle (-ing): “The window is breaking.”
  • In a condition → past participle (-ed/irregular): “The window is broken.”

The problem is compounding implies the problem is actively compounding something. That is not the intended meaning — the problem has been made worse. Therefore: compounded.

Thought Process — Present Perfect vs Past Simple

  • Use Present Perfect with: already, yet, ever, never, just, since, for, recently.
  • Use Past Simple with: yesterday, last week, in 2010, at 3pm, on Monday — any specific past time.

If a specific past time marker and have/has appear together in one clause — that is a T error.

3.1.3 Practice — Tense

  1. She walk to school every day.
  2. He has visit the museum last week.
  3. By the time she arrived, he left.
  4. The bridge is building across the river. (Describe a completed state.)
  5. They are enjoy the show right now.

  7. She already finished her homework. (Use a signal word for Present Perfect.)
  8. When the teacher entered, the students finished their test.

  10. The results were announcing last Friday.

3.2 Subject-Verb Agreement S

3.2.1 The Rule

A singular subject takes a singular verb. A plural subject takes a plural verb. The exam places extra words between the subject and the verb to make the wrong noun look like the subject. Your job is to strip those words out.

3.2.2 Identify the true subject

  1. Identify the main verb in the clause.
  2. Ask: who or what is doing or being this? That is the subject.
  3. Ignore everything between the subject and the verb.
  4. Is the true subject singular or plural? Match the verb accordingly.

Worked Example 1

The average age of caregivers are about 62.→The average age of caregivers is about 62.

Cover of caregivers. Remaining subject: the average age — singular.

Worked Example 2

Each of the boys have a book.→Each of the boys has a book.

Each is an indefinite pronoun and is always singular.

Worked Example 3

Neither the teacher nor the students was present.→Neither the teacher nor the students were present.

Compound subject with nor — apply the proximity rule. Closest subject: students (plural).

Thought Process — The Proximity Trap

Passages often place a plural noun immediately before the verb when the actual subject is singular. The nearest noun is the one the eye settles on, and that is the trap.

A practical method: cover the words between of (or a similar preposition) and the verb with your finger, then read what remains. The true subject becomes clear.

PatternRuleExample
Collective nounsgroup, team, committee, family — singular in formal writingThe committee has decided.
Indefinite pronounseach, every, either, neither, no one, someone, everyone — always singularEveryone has submitted.
‘A number of’ vs ‘The number of’A number of = plural; The number of = singularA number of students were late.
Or / norVerb agrees with the subject closest to itNeither he nor they were informed.
Relative clausesVerb agrees with the noun the clause refers toIt is the students who are responsible.

3.3 Article Errors A1

3.3.1 The Rule

The articles are a, an and the. The key distinction for a vs an is the sound the following word begins with — not the letter.

ArticleWhen to UseExamples
aSingular countable nouns beginning with a consonant sound.a book, a university (/j/), a one-way street (/w/), a European (/j/)
anSingular countable nouns beginning with a vowel sound.an apple, an hour (silent h), an honest man, an MBA (/em/)
theSpecific, already mentioned or uniquely identifiable nouns. Before superlatives.the book I mentioned, the tallest building, the sun
no articlePlural countable nouns used generally; uncountable nouns generally; proper nouns.Dogs are loyal. Water is essential. Singapore is small.

Thought Process — Sound, Not Spelling

  • University starts with U, but the first sound is like ‘y’ (consonant). → a university.
  • Hour starts with H, but H is silent. → an hour.
  • MBA — M is spoken ‘em’ (vowel sound). → an MBA.
  • European starts with E, but the first sound is like ‘y’. → a European.

Rule: say the word aloud in your head. Whatever sound comes first determines the article.

Worked Example 1

She is a honest person.→She is an honest person.

The H in honest is silent, so the first sound is a vowel.

Worked Example 2

He is an honest and a hardworking employee.→He is an honest and hardworking employee.

Both adjectives modify the same noun, so only one article is needed.

Worked Example 3

She is interested in a environment.→She is interested in the environment.

The environment is a specific, uniquely identifiable concept.

3.4 Pronoun Errors M

3.4.1 The Rule

A pronoun must agree with its antecedent in number, gender and person, and must be in the correct form for its role: subject, object, possessive or reflexive.

PersonSubjectObjectPossessiveReflexive
1st singularImemy / minemyself
2nd sing./pluralyouyouyour / yoursyourself / yourselves
3rd singular (m)hehimhishimself
3rd singular (f)sheherher / hersherself
3rd singular (n)itititsitself
1st pluralweusour / oursourselves
3rd pluraltheythemtheir / theirsthemselves

Worked Example 1 — Number

This patients are grateful for the subsidies.→These patients are grateful for the subsidies.

This is for singular nouns; these is for plural.

Worked Example 2 — Subject / Object Form

Me and my friend went to the library.→My friend and I went to the library.

Remove my friend: “Me went to the library” — obviously wrong. Convention: put yourself last.

Worked Example 3 — Reflexive Misuse

Please contact myself if you have questions.→Please contact me if you have questions.

Reflexive pronouns are only valid when subject and object are the same person.

Thought Process — Subject vs Object Form

The removal test: “The teacher gave the award to John and I.” → Remove John: “gave the award to I” → wrong → use me.

The preposition rule: after any preposition (to, for, with, between, from, at, by, of) always use the object form. Never I, he, she, we, they after a preposition.

Thought Process — Singular ‘They’

Indefinite pronouns (everyone, someone, anyone, each, every) are grammatically singular but refer to people of unknown or mixed gender. Modern formal English accepts singular they/their: “Everyone must bring their own pen.”

Caution: the verb still follows the singular antecedent (everyone has, not everyone have). In the exam, do not mark singular they as an error.

  6. Each student must complete their own project. (Accept singular ‘their’.)

3.5 Preposition Errors P

3.5.1 The Rule

A preposition error occurs when the wrong preposition is used with a verb, adjective or noun. These are fixed phrases (collocations): they are settled by usage rather than by rule, so each one has to be learnt individually.

Thought Process — Why Prepositions Cannot Be Guessed

Consider afflicted with. Why with and not by or from? There is no logical grammar reason — the word simply collocates with with. Reasoning from meaning alone will not produce the right answer.

Strategy: every time you meet a preposition error in practice, add the phrase to a personal list. A list built from your own mistakes is the most efficient way to revise this category.

3.6 Connector Errors C

3.6.1 The Rule

A connector error occurs when the wrong linking word is used, or when two conflicting connectors appear together. The connector must accurately reflect the logical relationship between the clauses it joins.

RelationshipConnectorsExample
Contrastbut, however, yet, although, even though, despite, neverthelessShe studied hard, but she failed.
Causebecause, since, as, due to, owing toShe passed because she studied.
Resultso, therefore, thus, hence, consequentlyShe studied hard; therefore, she passed.
Additionand, furthermore, moreover, in additionShe studied hard and revised her notes.
Conditionif, unless, provided that, as long asShe will pass if she studies.
Concessionalthough, even though, while, whereasAlthough unpopular, it was necessary.

Thought Process — ‘Although’ + ‘But’ Cannot Co-exist

This is the most commonly tested C error. Although signals concession; but also signals contrast. English allows only one connector per logical relationship.

  • “Although he was tired, he continued working.” Correct.
  • “He was tired, but he continued working.” Correct.
  • “Although he was tired, but he continued working.” Wrong.

The same logic applies to because + so. One logical relationship → one connector.

Thought Process — Comma Splice with ‘However’

However is a conjunctive adverb, not a coordinating conjunction. It cannot join two independent clauses with only a comma. “She studied hard, however she failed.” is a comma splice. Three fixes:

  • Semicolon: “She studied hard; however, she failed.”
  • Full stop: “She studied hard. However, she failed.”
  • Replace with but: “She studied hard, but she failed.”

The same rule applies to therefore, moreover, furthermore, consequently, nevertheless.

Correct PairCommon Error
not only … but alsonot only … and also
neither … norneither … or
either … oreither … nor
both … andboth … as well as
whether … orwhether … nor

Worked Examples

Although he was tired, but he continued working.→Although he was tired, he continued working.

He is not only hardworking, and also creative.→not only … but also

Despite he was ill, he came to school.→Despite being ill, he came to school. — Despite takes a noun or gerund, not a clause.

3.7 Word Form Errors — Adjective / Adverb A2

3.7.1 The Rule

An adjective modifies a noun. An adverb modifies a verb, adjective or another adverb. This is the most frequently tested error type. The error is almost always an adjective used where an adverb is needed.

  1. Find the word you are checking.
  2. Find what it is modifying, or what role it plays in the sentence.
  3. Apply the rule: modifies a noun → adjective; modifies a verb/adjective/adverb → adverb; is the subject or object → noun; expresses the action → verb.
  4. Convert to the correct form.

Worked Examples

He spoke confident in front of the class.→confidently — modifies the verb spoke.

… unable to cope mentally and physical …→physically — modifies cope, and must match mentally in parallel structure.

Her improve was remarkable.→improvement — the subject of a sentence must be a noun.

  • economic = relating to the economy | economical = thrifty

  • affect (verb, to influence) | effect (noun, a result)
  • practise (verb) | practice (noun)

3.8 Singular / Plural and Determiner Errors R

3.8.1 The Rule

R covers two related error types: (1) a noun in the wrong number; (2) a determiner or quantifier that does not match its noun. Do not confuse R with S. S is about the verb not agreeing with the subject. R is about the noun itself being in the wrong form, or its determiner being wrong.

Thought Process — Countable vs Uncountable

A countable noun can be counted: one book, two books. An uncountable noun cannot: information, advice, water.

  • Countable plural: many, few, several, a number of, these, those, both
  • Uncountable: much, little, a great deal of, a piece of

“Many informations” is wrong because information is uncountable. Correct: much information.

amount → uncountable: a large amount of water. number → countable plural: a large number of students.

Memory aid: if you can count it, use number. If you can only measure it, use amount.

3.9 Comparative and Superlative Errors D

3.9.1 The Rule

D errors involve using the wrong degree of comparison, or forming it incorrectly by combining both the -er/-est suffix and the word more/most on the same adjective. Three degrees: positive (base), comparative (two), superlative (three or more).

Adjective TypePositiveComparativeSuperlativeNote
One syllabletall, oldtaller, oldertallest, oldestDouble final consonant if short vowel + consonant: big → bigger
One syllable -ewide, safewider, saferwidest, safestAdd -r/-st only
Two syllables -yhappy, easyhappier, easierhappiest, easiestChange -y to -i
Two+ syllablesbeautifulmore beautifulmost beautifulNever add -er/-est
Irregulargood, bad, farbetter, worse, furtherbest, worst, furthestMemorise. Never add -er/-est or more/most

Thought Process — How Many Things Are Being Compared?

  • Exactly two → comparative (-er or more): “She is taller than her sister.”
  • Three or more → superlative (-est or most): “She is the tallest in the class.”

Double comparative / superlative: if you see both more and -er on the same adjective, or both most and -est — that is always an error.

Worked Examples

She is more taller than her sister.→taller — double comparative.

This is the most easiest question.→easiest — double superlative.

He is the most intelligent of the two.→the more intelligent — of the two signals exactly two.

3.9.3 Practice — Degree of Comparison

  1. Singapore is one of the most safest cities in the world.
  2. She is more smarter than her classmates by a significant margin.
  3. Of the two candidates, she is the most qualified for the role.
  4. He is the most intelligent of the two finalists.
  5. This method is more better than the previous one used.
  6. 25 per cent of Singaporeans will be 65 years and oldest by 2030.
  7. She is more happier than she was last year.
  8. This is the most simplest explanation I can provide.
  9. He runs more faster than anyone else on the team.
  10. Of all the students, she scored the more highly on the test.

6. Exam Tips

6.1 Timing Strategy

The editing question is worth 8 marks in Paper 1 and should take no more than 10 minutes. Pacing matters more than any single grammar tip.

StageTimeWhat to Do
First read2 minRead the passage for meaning. Identify the tense. Do not correct yet. Write S-T-A-M-P-C-A-R-D in the margin.
Line-by-line pass6 minAbout 36 seconds per numbered line. Run the checklist. Stop the moment you find one error.
Review pass1.5 minRe-check the two lines you marked ‘no error’. Re-check any line where you were uncertain.
Buffer0.5 minReserved for any line you flagged for return.

Timing self-test

During your last fortnight of revision, attempt editing passages with a stopwatch. If you cannot finish within 10 minutes, the problem is your method rather than your grammar. Practise the procedure before learning more rules.

6.2 Final Strategy Checklist

  1. Read for meaning first. The first read establishes the tense and topic, essential for T and C errors.
  2. Check T, S, A2 first on every line. These three account for the majority of errors. (A2 is word form — A1, articles, is tested less often.)
  3. Two lines are always correct. If STAMP CARD reveals nothing, tick it and move on.
  4. Write corrections clearly. Underline the error word; write the correction on the answer line. Ambiguous markings lose marks even when the answer is right.
  5. Each numbered line has at most one error. Once found, move on. Do not look for a second error on the same line.
  6. Memorise the high-yield fixed phrases (P). See the starred entries in 3.5.
  7. For D errors, count the things being compared. Two → comparative. Three or more → superlative.
  8. Do not guess on the first pass. If stuck after all nine letters, mark the line and return. A second pass under T, S or A2 almost always reveals the answer.
  9. Distinguish A1 from A2. A1 is Articles. A2 is word form. Two separate checks on every line.

Appendix A — Full Twelve-Tense Reference

The three tenses tested most often in 1184 editing are Past Simple, Present Simple and Present Perfect (see 3.1). This appendix lists all twelve for completeness.

TenseFormWhen to UseExample
Present Tenses
Present Simpleverb / verb+sHabits, routines, general truths.She walks.
Present Continuousam/is/are + -ingAction happening right now.She is walking.
Present Perfecthave/has + p.p.Past action with current relevance.She has walked.
Present Perfect Cont.have/has + been + -ingOngoing action from past until now.She has been walking.
Past Tenses
Past Simpleverb+ed / irregularCompleted action; narrative passages.She walked.
Past Continuouswas/were + -ingOngoing past action; background.She was walking.
Past Perfecthad + p.p.Earlier of two past events.She had walked.
Past Perfect Cont.had + been + -ingOngoing action before another past event.She had been walking.
Future Tenses
Future Simplewill + basePrediction, promise, decision.She will walk.
Future Continuouswill + be + -ingIn progress at a future moment.She will be walking.
Future Perfectwill + have + p.p.Completed before a future point.She will have walked.
Future Perfect Cont.will + have + been + -ingOngoing up to a future point.She will have been walking.

Thought Process — Why Only Three Tenses Matter for Editing

1184 editing passages are written in either present tense (factual) or past tense (narrative). The exam tests whether you can detect a verb that violates the established tense. In practice, three tenses do almost all the work: Past Simple dominates narrative passages, Present Simple dominates factual passages, and Present Perfect appears as a trap.
